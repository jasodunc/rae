### Series Abstract
Rational Architecture & Engineering (RAE) is a structural discipline dedicated to understanding and repairing the systems that make coherent reasoning, coordination, and action possible. The project began as Moral Architecture & Engineering (MAE), an attempt to understand suffering as an architectural problem. It expanded as the same underlying constraints proved applicable beyond morality: to epistemic systems, institutions, artificial agents, biological systems, and other complex architectures.

Rather than treating systemic failure primarily as a defect of individual values or intentions, RAE studies the architectural conditions that produce predictable failure. It identifies missing layers, maps recurring failure modes, examines the human-built patches that reduce harm, and develops the foundations for systems that are more coherent, stable, adaptive, and capable of repair.

The series unfolds in five volumes. Volume –1 introduces the conceptual scaffolding, vocabulary, and metaphors required to navigate the field. Volume 0 establishes the foundational architecture and preconditions beneath rational and moral systems. Volumes IA and IB diagnose structural failures in human and institutional morality, including the accumulated “strikes” against systems that claim moral perfection. Volume II presents principles and methods for constructing resilient moral systems. Volume III applies the architecture to historical, contemporary, and emerging contexts.

Together, these volumes develop a framework for treating reasoning, coordination, and morality not merely as collections of beliefs or prescriptions, but as architectural systems subject to constraints, failure modes, measurement, iteration, and repair.

### Series Preface

Rational Architecture & Engineering (RAE) began with a simple but unsettling observation: moral systems fail in predictable ways. They fail not because people are wicked or irrational, but because the systems themselves are built on fragile assumptions, missing layers, and unexamined architectural constraints. Across cultures and centuries, the same structural failures recur — in religion, governance, ethics, institutions, technology, and everyday life.

This series treats morality not as a set of values, but as a designed system with engineering requirements, failure modes, and substrate-level constraints. It approaches morality the way we approach software, infrastructure, or safety-critical systems: by asking what must be true for the system to function at all.

**Example:** When Hurricane Katrina struck New Orleans, the catastrophe wasn’t primarily about the storm—it was about missing architectural layers. No mandatory evacuation enforcement. Inadequate levee design. Failed communication systems. Poor resource coordination. Each represented a structural omission that a better-designed system would have included. Today, weather satellites, building codes, and emergency management systems partially patch these gaps. But the patches are incomplete, inconsistent, and arrived only after massive suffering. A morally competent architecture would have included them from the beginning.

This is what RAE studies: why the layers were missing, how humans are building partial approximations, and what a complete architecture would require.
